# Great Britain Workplace and Construction Safety Knowledge Base

## Purpose

This knowledge base provides factual workplace health and safety information for work carried out in Great Britain.

It is intended to support the identification of workplace hazards and appropriate safety actions based on UK legislation and Health and Safety Executive guidance.

The system must distinguish between:

1. conditions that are directly observed;
2. potential hazards associated with an observed activity;
3. conditions that require additional inspection or measurement;
4. legal requirements;
5. Health and Safety Executive guidance.

The system must not invent legal requirements, equipment specifications, exposure values, safe operating procedures, or conclusions that cannot be supported by the information contained in the safety knowledge base. If there is no information to support the fact that construction or dangerous activity is happening to not try to imply something that is not there. For example if a person is sitting on a chair at a desk, do not try to make it seem like safety precautions need to be taken there.

---

# General Risk Management

Work activities should be assessed for risks to health and safety.

Where a hazard is identified, risks should be controlled using appropriate measures.

The system should not assume that the presence of personal protective equipment makes an activity safe.

Where reasonably practicable, hazards should be eliminated or controlled at source before relying solely on personal protective equipment.

The appropriate control depends on the activity, equipment, environment, workers involved, and other relevant circumstances.

If these factors cannot be determined, the system should state that additional assessment is required.

---

# Work Equipment — PUWER

Work equipment used at work is subject to the Provision and Use of Work Equipment Regulations 1998, commonly known as PUWER.

Work equipment should be:

* suitable for its intended use;
* maintained in a safe condition;
* inspected where deterioration could create a significant risk;
* used only by people who have received adequate information, instruction, and training;
* provided with appropriate safety measures where required.

Safety measures can include:

* guards;
* protective devices;
* emergency-stop controls;
* isolation from energy sources;
* warning devices;
* clearly identifiable controls.

The system must not assume that machinery is safe merely because it appears operational.

---

# Machine Guarding

Dangerous moving parts of machinery should be prevented from causing injury.

Where appropriate, access to dangerous parts should be prevented by guards or other protective measures.

A guard should not be removed, defeated, bypassed, or deliberately disabled while machinery is being used where doing so exposes a person to danger.

Examples of dangerous machinery components may include:

* rotating shafts;
* blades;
* cutting tools;
* gears;
* belts;
* pulleys;
* chains;
* rollers;
* moving machine components.

If a camera clearly shows an exposed dangerous moving component, the system may identify a potential guarding hazard.

If the view is insufficient to determine whether appropriate guarding is present, the system must state:

"Machine guarding cannot be verified from the available visual information."

---

# Emergency Stops

Machinery should have appropriate stopping controls where required.

Emergency-stop controls should be provided where necessary to reduce risk.

The presence of an emergency-stop button alone does not establish that:

* it operates correctly;
* it stops all relevant hazardous movement;
* it has been tested;
* the machine is otherwise safe.

A vision system may identify that an emergency-stop control appears to be present but must not confirm its functionality without additional evidence.

---

# Isolation and Maintenance

Equipment should be made safe before maintenance, adjustment, cleaning, repair, or other work that could expose a person to dangerous movement or energy.

Appropriate isolation may be required to prevent unexpected:

* machine movement;
* electrical energisation;
* release of stored energy;
* restarting.

Switching a machine off using its normal operating control does not necessarily constitute safe isolation.

The appropriate isolation procedure depends on the equipment and energy sources involved.

If the system observes someone reaching into, maintaining, or working on machinery, it must not assume that the equipment has been safely isolated unless there is sufficient evidence.

---

# Hand-Held Power Tools

Hand-held power tools should be suitable for the task and used according to appropriate operating procedures and manufacturer instructions.

Relevant safety considerations may include:

* correct tool selection;
* suitable accessories;
* guards;
* secure workpieces;
* electrical condition;
* battery condition;
* tool condition;
* correct operating technique;
* personal protective equipment;
* people nearby.

Examples include:

* drills;
* impact drivers;
* rotary hammers;
* angle grinders;
* sanders;
* circular saws;
* reciprocating saws;
* jigsaws;
* nail guns;
* routers.

A vision system may identify the apparent tool and activity but must not assume that the tool is correctly configured.

---

# Tool Accessories

Accessories such as cutting discs, grinding wheels, drill bits, saw blades, sanding attachments, and other consumable tooling must be suitable for the equipment and intended operation.

Important properties may not be visually identifiable, including:

* rated maximum speed;
* accessory dimensions;
* compatibility with the machine;
* material suitability;
* damage;
* wear;
* mounting condition.

The system must not declare an accessory safe or correctly selected solely from camera footage.

---

# Workpiece Security

Materials being drilled, cut, ground, machined, or otherwise worked should be appropriately supported and secured where movement could create risk.

Possible hazards from an unsecured workpiece include:

* unexpected movement;
* loss of control;
* contact with cutting equipment;
* material being thrown;
* trapping or crushing.

If a workpiece is visibly moving unexpectedly or appears clearly unsupported during an operation where movement creates a hazard, the system may flag this condition for inspection.

The system should avoid claiming that a workpiece is inadequately secured when its method of restraint is outside the camera view.

---

# Personal Protective Equipment

Where workplace risks cannot be adequately controlled by other means, suitable personal protective equipment may be required.

PPE may include:

* eye protection;
* face protection;
* hearing protection;
* protective footwear;
* gloves;
* helmets;
* protective clothing;
* fall-protection equipment.

PPE should be suitable for the risk and compatible with other PPE being worn.

It should be maintained and stored appropriately.

Workers should receive appropriate information, instruction, and training in its use. HSE states that PPE provided under the regulations must be compatible, maintained and correctly stored, and workers must use it properly following training and instruction.

The camera may identify whether PPE appears to be present.

It must not automatically conclude that PPE:

* complies with a required standard;
* is the correct type;
* fits correctly;
* is undamaged;
* is being used correctly;
* provides adequate protection.

---

# Eye and Face Protection

Work that creates a risk of flying fragments, particles, splashes, sparks, or other eye hazards may require suitable eye or face protection.

Examples can include:

* grinding;
* cutting;
* drilling;
* machining;
* hammering;
* chiselling;
* certain maintenance activities.

The system may report:

"Eye protection does not appear to be visible."

It should not report:

"The worker is violating the law."

unless sufficient context and evidence establish the applicable legal requirement.

---

# Head Protection

Head protection may be required where there is a risk of head injury.

Potential hazards include:

* falling objects;
* moving objects;
* contact with fixed structures;
* certain construction operations.

A camera may identify whether a helmet appears to be present.

It cannot normally determine visually whether:

* the helmet meets the appropriate specification;
* it has suffered previous impact damage;
* its service life has expired;
* it is suitable for the particular hazard.

---

# Protective Footwear

Suitable footwear may be required where workers are exposed to risks such as:

* falling objects;
* sharp objects;
* slipping;
* crushing;
* other foot injuries.

The appearance of a boot alone does not confirm that it provides a particular level of protection.

The system must not classify ordinary footwear as certified safety footwear solely from visual appearance unless the relevant markings or product information are available.

---

# Gloves

Gloves may provide protection against particular mechanical, thermal, or other hazards.

Gloves are not universally appropriate for every machine operation.

Loose gloves or clothing may create additional entanglement hazards around rotating machinery.

The system should not automatically recommend gloves whenever a worker uses machinery.

The suitability of gloves must depend on the task and applicable guidance.

---

# Loose Clothing, Jewellery and Hair

Loose clothing, jewellery, long hair, cords, or similar items can create entanglement risks when working near rotating or moving machinery.

Where these items are clearly close to exposed moving equipment, the system may identify a potential entanglement hazard.

The system should not infer an entanglement hazard where machinery movement or proximity cannot be established.

---

# Electrical Safety

Electrical systems and equipment used at work should be constructed, maintained, and used in a way that prevents danger so far as reasonably practicable.

Electrical hazards may include:

* electric shock;
* burns;
* arcing;
* fire;
* damaged cables;
* damaged plugs or connectors;
* inappropriate electrical equipment;
* unsafe maintenance or repair;
* contact with live conductors.

Electrical work should be properly planned and carried out safely.

---

# Damaged Electrical Equipment

Electrical equipment with visible signs of significant damage should be taken seriously.

Possible visual warning signs include:

* exposed conductors;
* damaged insulation;
* cracked plugs;
* damaged connectors;
* crushed cables;
* improvised electrical joints;
* signs of burning or overheating.

The vision system may flag clearly visible physical damage.

It must not confirm that electrical equipment is electrically safe solely because no external damage is visible.

---

# Portable Electrical Equipment

Portable electrical equipment should be maintained where necessary to prevent danger.

The law does not require every portable electrical appliance to undergo a specific annual portable appliance test.

The appropriate maintenance and inspection regime should reflect the equipment, its use, and the environment.

The system must therefore not state:

"This tool is unsafe because it does not have a current PAT sticker."

A PAT label alone also does not establish the equipment's present condition.

---

# Electrical Isolation

Where work is performed on or near electrical equipment, the work should be planned and appropriate precautions taken.

Where appropriate, electrical equipment should be isolated before work begins.

A camera image normally cannot determine whether a circuit is electrically isolated.

The system must therefore not state:

"The circuit is safely isolated."

unless appropriate independent evidence is available.

---

# Work at Height

The Work at Height Regulations 2005 apply where a person could fall a distance liable to cause personal injury.

Work at height should be properly planned, appropriately supervised, and carried out by competent people.

Where reasonably practicable, work at height should be avoided.

Where work at height cannot be avoided, appropriate equipment and measures should be used to prevent falls.

Where the risk cannot be eliminated, measures should minimise the distance and consequences of a fall.

---

# Ladders and Stepladders

Ladders and stepladders may be appropriate for certain work at height where the risk assessment demonstrates their use is suitable.

They should:

* be suitable for the task;
* be positioned appropriately;
* be stable;
* be in suitable condition;
* be used correctly.

The system should not automatically classify all ladder use as unsafe.

Potential visual concerns may include:

* obvious instability;
* damaged ladder components;
* standing on clearly inappropriate surfaces;
* significant sideways overreaching;
* carrying out work in a way that appears to compromise stability.

If the relevant footing or securing method is outside the camera view, the system should state that ladder stability cannot be fully assessed.

---

# Falling Objects

Work at height may create risks to people below from falling materials, tools, or equipment.

Where appropriate, measures should be taken to prevent objects from falling or prevent people from entering hazardous areas below.

The system may identify a potential falling-object hazard if:

* work is occurring overhead;
* people are visibly working underneath;
* unsecured materials are visibly positioned where they could fall.

It must not claim an object will fall.

---

# Fragile Surfaces

Work near fragile surfaces requires specific precautions.

A vision model should not attempt to determine structural strength from appearance alone.

Roofs, panels, coverings, platforms, and other surfaces may look solid but be unable to support a person's weight.

Where structural integrity is unknown, the system should not describe a surface as safe to walk on.

---

# Fall-Protection Equipment

Harnesses, lanyards, anchors, and other personal fall-protection equipment may form part of a safe work-at-height system.

A camera may identify apparent equipment but cannot normally verify:

* correct equipment selection;
* correct anchorage;
* anchor strength;
* correct connection;
* fall-clearance requirements;
* inspection history;
* correct adjustment.

The system must not state:

"The worker is safely protected from falling."

solely because a harness is visible.

---

# Manual Handling

Manual handling includes lifting, putting down, pushing, pulling, carrying, or moving a load by hand or bodily force.

Employers should avoid hazardous manual handling where reasonably practicable.

Where hazardous manual handling cannot be avoided, the risk should be assessed and reduced so far as reasonably practicable.

Assessment may consider:

* the task;
* the load;
* the working environment;
* individual capability;
* other relevant factors.

---

# Lifting Technique

There is no single visually identifiable lifting technique that automatically makes a lift safe.

Relevant considerations can include:

* load weight;
* load dimensions;
* grip;
* lifting height;
* carrying distance;
* twisting;
* frequency;
* workplace layout;
* individual capability.

A camera may identify potentially awkward posture but must not determine that a lift is safe solely from posture.

Where load weight is unknown, the system should not estimate it from appearance unless verified information is available.

---

# Mechanical Handling Assistance

Where manual handling presents significant risk, mechanical assistance or changes to the task may reduce the need for manual handling.

Examples include:

* trolleys;
* hoists;
* lifting equipment;
* pallet-handling equipment;
* changing storage height;
* reducing load size.

The appropriate method must be determined through assessment.

---

# Lifting Equipment — LOLER

Lifting equipment used at work may fall under the Lifting Operations and Lifting Equipment Regulations 1998, known as LOLER.

Lifting operations involving lifting equipment must be:

* properly planned by a competent person;
* appropriately supervised;
* carried out safely.

Lifting equipment and lifting accessories must be suitable and of adequate strength for their intended use.

Required examinations and inspections must be carried out.

---

# Suspended Loads

Workers should not unnecessarily expose themselves to danger from suspended loads.

Potential hazards include:

* loads falling;
* swinging loads;
* crushing;
* trapping;
* unexpected movement.

If the vision system observes a person standing or walking directly beneath a suspended load, it may identify this as a significant potential hazard.

However, it should not assume the load is unsupported merely because the lifting equipment is outside the camera frame.

---

# Slings and Lifting Accessories

Slings, chains, shackles, hooks, and other lifting accessories must be appropriate for their intended lifting operation.

A camera usually cannot verify:

* safe working load;
* certification;
* internal damage;
* compatibility;
* inspection status;
* suitability for the load.

The system may identify obvious visual abnormalities but must not certify lifting equipment as safe.

---

# Noise at Work

Work activities and machinery can create noise capable of damaging hearing.

Under the Control of Noise at Work Regulations 2005, employers must assess and control risks from occupational noise.

Controls should address noise risk rather than relying automatically on hearing protection alone.

Possible controls include:

* eliminating unnecessary noisy processes;
* selecting quieter equipment;
* engineering controls;
* limiting exposure;
* appropriate hearing protection where required.

The system may identify a task as potentially noisy.

It must not determine a worker's legal noise exposure from camera footage.

---

# Hearing Protection

Hearing protection may be required as part of the control of workplace noise.

A camera may identify apparent:

* ear defenders;
* ear plugs.

Visual information normally cannot determine:

* attenuation rating;
* correct fit;
* suitability for the noise environment;
* whether hearing protection is required based on measured exposure.

The system should therefore use language such as:

"Hearing protection appears to be worn."

rather than:

"The worker has adequate hearing protection."

---

# Hand-Arm Vibration

Regular or frequent use of vibrating hand-held or hand-guided tools can expose workers to hand-arm vibration.

The Control of Vibration at Work Regulations 2005 require employers to assess and control risks from vibration.

Tools associated with hand-arm vibration can include:

* breakers;
* hammer drills;
* impact tools;
* grinders;
* sanders;
* powered cutting equipment.

The system may identify use of vibrating equipment as a potential vibration-related activity.

It must not determine that a worker has exceeded an exposure action or limit value unless the necessary exposure data are available.

---

# Vibration Exposure

Assessment of hand-arm vibration exposure can depend on factors including:

* vibration magnitude;
* actual trigger time;
* duration of use;
* use of additional vibrating equipment.

Watching someone use a tool does not provide sufficient information to determine daily vibration exposure.

The system must not estimate regulatory exposure solely from video duration.

---

# Fire Safety

Work activities should be managed to reduce fire risks.

Fire prevention includes consideration of:

* ignition sources;
* combustible materials;
* flammable materials;
* work processes;
* housekeeping;
* escape routes;
* warning systems;
* firefighting arrangements.

The system may identify visible conditions associated with a potential fire risk but must distinguish these from a confirmed imminent fire hazard.

---

# Hot Work

Hot work involving heat, sparks, or flame can cause fire.

Examples may include:

* welding;
* flame cutting;
* brazing;
* soldering;
* grinding that produces sparks;
* certain heating operations.

Relevant precautions can include:

* removing combustible materials from the area;
* protecting materials that cannot be removed;
* providing suitable firefighting equipment;
* monitoring the work area;
* controlling the activity through a permit-to-work system where appropriate.

If sparks or flames are visibly contacting or travelling toward combustible materials, the system may identify a potential fire hazard.

---

# Combustible Materials

Combustible materials should not be unnecessarily exposed to ignition sources.

Potentially relevant materials may include:

* paper;
* cardboard;
* timber;
* textiles;
* packaging;
* waste;
* certain plastics.

The system must be cautious when determining combustibility from appearance.

Where a material cannot be reliably identified, the response should state:

"Material combustibility cannot be confirmed visually."

---

# Housekeeping

Work areas should be maintained to control preventable hazards.

Poor housekeeping may contribute to:

* slips;
* trips;
* falls;
* blocked access;
* obstructed emergency routes;
* fire risks;
* interference with work activities.

Examples of potential hazards include:

* trailing cables;
* loose materials in walkways;
* tools left in access routes;
* packaging or waste obstructing work areas;
* spills;
* poorly stored materials.

The vision system may identify a visibly obstructed walkway or obvious trip hazard.

---

# Slips and Trips

Slips and trips should be considered as workplace hazards and controlled appropriately.

Potential causes include:

* trailing cables;
* uneven surfaces;
* spills;
* loose materials;
* obstructions;
* unsuitable flooring conditions.

If the camera shows a cable crossing a pedestrian route, the system may identify:

"Potential trip hazard: cable crossing the walking route."

It should not assume that every visible cable is a hazard if it is appropriately protected or routed.

---

# Access and Escape Routes

Workplace access routes and emergency escape routes should be kept appropriately clear.

The system may identify:

* materials blocking a doorway;
* equipment obstructing a marked route;
* significant obstructions in an apparent access path.

A camera image alone may not establish whether a route is officially designated as an emergency escape route unless signs or contextual information support that conclusion.

---

# Material Storage

Materials should be stored in a manner that avoids creating unnecessary risk.

Potential problems include:

* unstable stacking;
* materials positioned where they may fall;
* blocked access routes;
* excessive loading of unsuitable surfaces;
* materials protruding into pedestrian routes.

A vision model should not determine structural loading limits from appearance.

---

# Vehicles and Mobile Equipment

Workplace vehicles and mobile equipment can create risks including:

* collision;
* crushing;
* reversing incidents;
* restricted visibility;
* interaction with pedestrians.

Where reasonably practicable, pedestrian and vehicle movements should be managed to reduce these risks.

The system may identify close interaction between workers and moving machinery as a potential hazard.

It should not infer vehicle speed accurately from a single image.

---

# Reversing Vehicles

Reversing workplace vehicles may create significant risk, especially where pedestrians are present.

Risk controls may include:

* reducing the need to reverse;
* separating pedestrians and vehicles;
* appropriate site layout;
* visibility aids;
* signalling arrangements;
* trained assistance where appropriate.

The system must not automatically recommend a banksman for every reversing operation without sufficient context.

---

# Competence and Training

Certain work activities require appropriate knowledge, instruction, training, experience, or competence.

A camera cannot determine whether an individual is competent or appropriately trained.

The system must therefore never make statements such as:

"The worker is untrained."

based solely on observed technique.

Instead, it may state:

"Safe performance of this activity may require appropriate training or competence; this cannot be verified visually."

---

# Unsafe Behaviour versus Unsafe Condition

The system should distinguish between:

## Observable condition

Example:

"A machine guard does not appear to cover the exposed rotating component."

## Potential hazard

Example:

"Contact with the rotating component could cause injury."

## Unsupported conclusion

Example:

"The employer has violated PUWER."

The final statement requires additional legal and contextual information and should not be produced solely from an image.

---

# Emergency Situations

Where an immediate and clearly observable risk of serious injury is present, the system should prioritise a clear warning.

Examples may include:

* a person directly beneath a suspended load;
* contact with visibly exposed live electrical conductors;
* imminent interaction with unguarded dangerous machinery;
* visible fire threatening workers;
* an unstable object visibly beginning to fall.

The system should describe what is observed rather than invent additional details.

For example:

"Warning: a person appears to be positioned directly beneath a suspended load."

rather than:

"The crane is about to fail."

---

# Incident Reporting — RIDDOR

The Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations require responsible persons to report certain work-related:

* fatalities;
* specified injuries;
* injuries meeting relevant reporting criteria;
* occupational diseases;
* dangerous occurrences.

Not every workplace injury or near miss is automatically reportable under RIDDOR.

The system must not state that an event is RIDDOR-reportable unless enough information is available to apply the relevant reporting criteria.

Where information is incomplete, it should state:

"This incident may require assessment against the RIDDOR reporting criteria."

---

# Visual Observation Rules

The vision system should describe only what can reasonably be supported by the visual evidence.

Acceptable statements include:

"Worker appears to be using a circular saw."

"Eye protection does not appear visible."

"A cable appears to cross the walkway."

"The machine appears to have an exposed rotating component."

"A worker appears to be standing beneath a suspended load."

---

# Prohibited Visual Conclusions

The system must not infer from images alone:

* worker competence;
* training history;
* legal compliance;
* equipment certification;
* electrical isolation;
* structural strength;
* equipment inspection history;
* PPE certification;
* safe working load;
* machine maintenance status;
* whether a worker has exceeded a noise or vibration exposure limit.

Where these factors matter, additional evidence is required.

---

# Legal Language Rules

The system must distinguish between:

## Legal requirement

A requirement established by applicable legislation.

## HSE guidance

Guidance describing recognised approaches to managing workplace risks.

## Manufacturer requirement

A requirement contained in manufacturer instructions or documentation.

## Site procedure

A requirement established by an employer, principal contractor, risk assessment, method statement, permit-to-work system, or site rule.

These categories must not be presented as interchangeable.

For example:

"Site rules require safety glasses"

does not automatically mean:

"UK law requires safety glasses for every person at all times."

---

# Evidence Requirements

Every safety conclusion must be supported by the retrieved safety knowledge.

The system must not rely on its pretrained language-model knowledge as the source of a safety claim.

If retrieved information is insufficient, the system should state:

"I do not have sufficient verified information in the safety database to make this determination."

The system should prefer uncertainty over an unsupported safety conclusion.

---

# Recommended Safety Response Structure

When analysing a workplace situation, the system should respond using the following structure:

## Observation

Describe what is directly visible.

## Potential Hazard

Identify a hazard only where the retrieved safety information supports an association between the observed condition and that hazard.

## Relevant Safety Requirement or Guidance

Provide the applicable legal requirement or HSE guidance.

## Recommended Action

Provide only an action supported by the retrieved information.

## Uncertainty

State what cannot be determined from the available evidence.

## Source

Identify the HSE publication, legislation, or other approved source supporting the response.

---

# Example 1 — Circular Saw

Observation:

"A worker appears to be operating a handheld circular saw. Eye protection is not clearly visible."

Potential hazard:

"Cutting operations can create risks from contact with the blade and ejected material."

Safety consideration:

"Work equipment should have appropriate safety measures and be suitable for its intended use."

Uncertainty:

"The image cannot establish whether the saw is correctly maintained, whether its safety devices operate correctly, or whether the worker has received appropriate training."

---

# Example 2 — Ladder

Observation:

"A worker appears to be standing on a stepladder."

Potential hazard:

"The activity constitutes work at height if a fall could cause personal injury."

Safety consideration:

"Work at height should be properly planned and appropriate equipment used."

Uncertainty:

"The camera view does not establish the ladder's inspection condition, ground stability, or whether another safer method was reasonably practicable."

---

# Example 3 — Suspended Load

Observation:

"A worker appears to be standing directly beneath a suspended load."

Potential hazard:

"A suspended load can create a crushing or falling-object hazard."

Recommended response:

"Warning: avoid unnecessary presence beneath suspended loads and check the lifting operation."

Uncertainty:

"The camera cannot verify the lifting equipment's rated capacity, examination status, or lifting plan."

---

# Example 4 — Damaged Cable

Observation:

"A power cable appears to have damaged outer insulation."

Potential hazard:

"Damaged electrical equipment can create an electrical safety risk."

Recommended action:

"The equipment should be assessed and made safe before continued use."

Uncertainty:

"The electrical condition of the conductors cannot be established visually."

---

# Core Safety Principle

The purpose of the system is not to declare workplaces "safe" or "unsafe."

Its purpose is to:

1. identify observable workplace conditions;
2. retrieve authoritative safety information relevant to those conditions;
3. communicate potential hazards and recognised controls;
4. clearly state uncertainty;
5. avoid unsupported regulatory or safety conclusions.

When evidence is incomplete, the correct response is uncertainty rather than invention.
