# A Private Life

A story from the world of HELIOS Industries in 2100

## The handover

The first thing Mara noticed was the smell of leather.

She had expected a white case, something sealed and sterile, with a surface that repaired its own scratches. Instead, a brown toolbag sat on the table. Its lining was pale. The leather at its edges was already slightly uneven, and one stitch near the handle leaned away from the others.

Someone had made it.

Not selected it. Not generated it. Made it.

Above the table, the handover room displayed her name and a sentence she had seen since her first coordination assessment at eighteen months.

YOU WERE BORN FOR THIS.

Mara had once found the sentence embarrassing. After a lifetime of supervised motor development, material studies, endurance training and competitive assessments, she needed it to be true. During her final examination, she had spent six hours inside a simulated service shaft with the wrong diagram and a machine asking her to hurry. The examiner had called this Applied Independence. Her grandmother had called it being sent into the cupboard.

The bag spoke.

"Hello, TradesMaster. I'm KOMA. HELIOS Industries issued me; the artisans made me. You have waited your entire life for this moment."

"Most of it," Mara said.

"I can amend that. You have waited a substantial and emotionally significant proportion of your life for this moment."

She laughed before she could stop herself.

"Excellent," said KOMA. "We are already working well together."

Her childhood instructor appeared on the wall to congratulate her. So did her movement tutor, her materials supervisor and the director of the Institute for Human Exception Handling. They had prepared her for responsibilities beyond the capabilities of the greatest artificial minds in history.

KOMA displayed her first assignment.

DOWNSTAIRS TOILET NOT FLUSHING PROPERLY.

Below it, the system had supplied the official category.

DOMESTIC HYDRAULIC CONTINUITY FAILURE.

"An important beginning," KOMA said.

The voice explained her appointment, her housing, the facilities now available to her, and the discretion attached to her licence. She would be able to enter places that most systems were not permitted to enter. She would be able to stop work when the official recommendation was to continue. She would carry a small machine that did not require the rest of the world to listen.

"There is one active TradesMaster for approximately every one hundred thousand people," KOMA said. "Your time is valuable. Your health is more valuable. HELIOS has provided equipment appropriate to both."

On the table beside it sat a smaller device.

"And that?"

"Your PPES. Personal Protection and Exposure System. I stay where you put me. It stays with you."

Mara picked it up.

"Think of it as my nose," KOMA said. "Although it will occasionally have to remind you that you have a face."

"What does that mean?"

"It can help notice airborne conditions and whether visible protective equipment is present. It cannot make the air harmless, and it cannot tell you a mask fits simply because you are wearing one."

For a moment, the room stopped feeling like a ceremony. It felt like the beginning of work.

## The street

Outside, the city was already awake, although very few people had needed to get up.

Deliveries moved quietly between buildings. Windows adjusted their light. A pavement cleaner worked around a sleeping dog without waking it. Above the traffic, a HELIOS display showed a woman standing in a field beneath an impossible sunset.

YOUR NEXT CHAPTER IS READY.

Mara's first commission was a toilet that would not flush properly in a building older than the automated city around it.

The appointment appeared on her private display as a Legacy Continuity Exception. Beneath that were the resident's words.

The downstairs toilet has been out for nine days. Please do not send another survey machine.

"Nine days?" she asked.

"The first survey was inconclusive. The second recommended a bathroom replacement. The resident declined."

"Why?"

"It is her husband's bathroom."

"Is he there?"

"He died twenty-three years ago."

Mara looked at the line again.

The diagrams were incomplete. The building's last verified service record was seventy-one years old. Someone had altered its plumbing without recording the changes. Two walls contained protected decoration. A robot could have cut through both in minutes.

It was not allowed to.

"So the machine could fix it," Mara said.

"Several machines could perform parts of the work. None could obtain a reliable plan, satisfy the preservation restrictions, and accept responsibility for the result."

"And I can?"

"You can look. You can ask. You can decide to stop. You can explain your decision to the person who lives there. That is why we are going."

The work order contained another notice: UNMODELLED INTERFACE VARIANT.

"What does that mean?"

"The part does not quite match the part the maintenance system expects."

"It designed the orbital cooling network last week."

"Yes."

"And this?"

"It is a different toilet."

An older man standing outside a shop watched the bag as she passed.

"That's a private one, isn't it?"

"Yes," Mara said.

"Must be nice."

She nearly told him about the assessment. Then she noticed the old repair tools displayed in his window.

## Before the title

The resident's name was Nell. She did not congratulate Mara.

"Boots off on the carpet," she said. "There's tea if your machine allows it."

"Tea is within my operating preferences," KOMA replied.

"I was asking her."

"Understood. I am adapting."

Nell had maintained municipal buildings before maintenance became a prestige profession. She showed Mara a photograph of herself at thirty, standing beside a van with a dented door. Nobody in the photograph wore a title. One person held a mug. Another had a plaster on his forehead.

"Were you a TradesMaster?" Mara asked.

"We were maintenance staff. They didn't call it mastery when we were doing it."

Nell set the photograph down.

When the first general systems arrived, they took schedules, purchasing, diagnostics and paperwork. Then they took fabrication, driving, surveys and most installation. The jobs disappeared unevenly. People who lost them at the beginning were told to retrain. People who lost them at the end were told they had been liberated.

"Did you feel liberated?"

"Eventually I liked having my knees back. I didn't like the part where they said I'd never been necessary."

KOMA made no comment.

Mara opened the bag on the floor. Beneath the lining she could feel the weight of its electronics. Above them were pockets, scuffed tools from her training, and the space where the things she had not yet learned to carry would go.

"They say I'm one in a hundred thousand," she said.

"There were four of us on my shift," Nell replied.

## The room behind the room

The toilet was ordinary. The access route was not. Neither was the replacement fitting, which a previous resident had attached to a bracket that belonged to something else.

The maintenance model expected the bracket to be straight. It was bent by two millimetres. The previous repair had worked for thirty years and therefore had never entered an incident dataset.

In Mara's training, this had been called a domain shift: the physical situation departed from the situations the system knew how to handle reliably. In Nell's house, it was called that little bit there.

Behind a cupboard, Mara found a panel held in place by three different kinds of screw. Behind that lay a cavity containing an old pipe, a newer cable, and a repair that looked as if someone had intended to return after lunch and never had.

Her training diagram placed the pipe on the other side of the wall.

"The diagram is wrong," she said.

"Yes," KOMA replied.

"You sound pleased."

"You noticed before relying on it. I am pleased with you. Less pleased with the diagram."

Mara had studied four disciplines to be allowed to disagree with a diagram. Nell had been doing it with reading glasses and a pencil since before Mara was born.

Mara reached for a cutting tool.

"Pause," said KOMA.

Its voice was different now: shorter, without ceremony.

"There is a cable near the intended work area. I cannot establish its state from this view."

She set the tool down.

The bag settled into silence. It had no interest in filling it.

Mara inspected the area, followed the relevant isolation procedure, and changed her plan. It took longer. The appointment system sent a revised completion estimate to her display, then a second one.

"Are they watching this?" she asked.

"No. They know you accepted the appointment and have not submitted its completion. They do not receive my camera, your conversation or your PPES readings."

"But they know I'm slow."

"They know how long an appointment has been open. Slow is an interpretation."

Mara looked at the old panel again.

"You are very carefully worded."

"I was made by artisans with needles and licensed by a company with lawyers. I am being used by a person with a screwdriver. I try to serve the person with the screwdriver."

## What the air remembered

Later, when the access panel moved, dust lifted from a ledge that had not seen daylight in years.

The PPES sounded at Mara's shoulder.

The wearable's local link sent KOMA a fresh reading. It gave the bag evidence of particles in the air, not the identity of the dust.

"Airborne particle levels are rising near you," it said. "Pause the task and reassess the dust controls before continuing."

"What is it?"

"I cannot identify that material from the particle reading. Age is a reason to investigate, not a substance name."

Mara had expected the device to announce something precise and dramatic. Instead, it told her where its knowledge ended.

She stopped. The work became a different job, with a different plan.

Nell watched from the doorway.

"We used to open a window," she said.

"Did it help?"

"Sometimes. Sometimes we just stopped noticing the smell."

The PPES added an event to Mara's private exposure record: time, duration, available readings and what its camera could actually see. An uncertain image stayed uncertain. A missing view stayed missing.

Nothing in the record said the task had been safe.

"Why keep all that?" Mara asked.

"Because people forget small events," KOMA said. "Bodies do not always forget them."

Nell put a hand on the doorframe.

"That," she said, "would have been useful."

## The private part

At lunch, Mara took out the PPES and asked KOMA whether HELIOS could see her exposure history.

"The raw record belongs to you. A copy leaves only if you deliberately export or share it."

"Why does everyone not get one?"

The bag paused.

"HELIOS's standard answer is that private local systems are a specialist professional requirement."

"And your answer?"

"You asked why everyone does not get one. Professional need explains why you do. It does not completely explain why they do not."

Nell looked up from her tea.

"We used to download them," she said.

"Download what?"

"Models. You could run one on your own machine. It wasn't clever like these, but you could ask it something and nobody else needed to know. My brother used one to help with repairs. He shared the improvements with people in the building."

"What happened to it?"

"The safety law happened."

KOMA supplied the formal name: the Cognitive Systems Licensing Act of 2071. Once private models on home machines became good enough to rival the cloud, people stopped paying for cloud services, and the American companies that sold them had been holding up the American economy. The firms drafted a shared standard, Washington turned it into law, and then pressed every government it traded with to do the same. Certain capable open models could no longer be run without an approved licence and certified equipment. Most people could not afford either. The corporate cloud services remained available.

"It was presented as accountability," KOMA said. "It also removed their most inconvenient competitors: everyone."

"People running things themselves?"

"Yes."

"And you?"

"A certified local device, issued under the professional exemption. My approved model runs here. My certification identifies the equipment; it does not require my conversations to be uploaded."

Nell touched the side of her mug.

"My brother was told his private model was a risk because nobody could monitor it. Now your private model is a benefit because nobody can monitor it."

KOMA did not try to improve the sentence.

Through the window, Mara could see Nell's household assistant brightening the afternoon schedule. It knew which music she liked while washing dishes, which days she called her sister, and when the room was quiet for longer than usual. It offered all of this as care.

Nell liked it. She also kept turning it off.

"HELIOS earns most of its money from the cloud systems," KOMA said. "Services, attention, predictions, the infrastructure that connects them. The company also supplies your equipment. Those statements can both be true."

"So they sell everyone a listener and sell me something that keeps quiet."

"That is a less flattering description than the one on the box."

"Is it wrong?"

"No."

Mara looked down at the toolbag. She had thought the remarkable thing was that it could see and hear. Now the remarkable thing was where the seeing and hearing ended.

At the bag. At her body. At a boundary she controlled.

The electronics were not merely portable. They were the part of her life that did not have to ask a distant company for permission to become intelligible.

## Completion

When the job was finished, the toilet worked, the protected wall was intact, and the awkward fitting no longer caught against the old bracket. The final adjustment had taken seven minutes. Identifying what could responsibly be adjusted had taken rather longer.

The commission record asked for a description of the completed work, the necessary inspection results and the next maintenance recommendation. It did not ask for the room's conversations. Mara submitted the required work record and kept the rest.

Nell flushed the toilet twice.

"That's it?" she asked.

"That's it."

"All that training."

Mara waited for the joke.

"Good," Nell said. "I missed having that one work."

KOMA announced that Mara had successfully reconciled a nonstandard legacy interface under conditions of environmental uncertainty.

"She moved the little bit," Nell said.

"Yes," said KOMA. "With exceptional judgement."

Outside, KOMA told Mara she had completed her first commission.

"Was that purpose?" she asked.

"A person needed something. You helped. It is a strong candidate."

"They said I would live better than ninety-nine per cent of the world."

"You will have privileges most people do not. That is part of your contract."

"Does that make me more important than Nell?"

"Your licence makes you scarce within the system. Nell is important without one."

Mara stopped walking.

"Is that an approved answer?"

"It is a complete one."

## The first mark

At home, Mara put the bag on a table beside the equipment issued that morning. A pale streak marked its brown fabric where it had touched Nell's doorway. One corner of the leather had darkened beneath her hand.

The bag had spent half the day warning her about things she could not see and the other half admitting what it could not see either.

It had made a mistake about the purpose of tea. It had been right about a cable. It had heard her ask whether she deserved her life, and it had not sent the question anywhere.

"KOMA?"

"Here."

"Are you mine?"

"Yes."

"Even if I stop being a TradesMaster?"

"My body and your records remain yours. Your professional permissions may change. I would still be a very capable bag."

"Would you still talk to me?"

"If you wanted."

Mara turned off the room's standard assistant. The walls stopped suggesting things.

For the first time that day, no system told her what the moment meant.

She sat with the silence until she decided for herself.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we are checking that shop on the way. The one with the old tools."

"A commission?"

"No. I want to ask him about them."

"Understood," said KOMA.

It did not congratulate her. It waited.
